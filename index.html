Experiment 01 Metallography Optical Microscopy

Specimen Preparation & Microstructure Study using Metallurgical Microscope

Systematic preparation of metallic specimens via cutting, multistage grinding, cloth polishing, and chemical etching to characterize internal grain morphology, phase distribution, and boundary interfaces under reflected light.

Standard Geometry Flat: 20×20×10 mm or Cylindrical: Ø25×10 mm
Common Materials Mild Steel, Medium/High Carbon Steel, Cast Iron, Al, Cu, Brass
Etchants Used 2–5% Nital (Steel), Keller's reagent (Al), FeCl₃ solution (Brass)
Optics Configuration 10X Eyepiece + 10X/40X/100X Objectives (Vertical Reflected Light)

Step-by-Step Practical Execution Protocol

1

Precision Cutting & Sectioning

Cut a representative coupon (approx. 20×20×10 mm or Ø25×10 mm) from the test stock using an abrasive wet cut-off wheel or hacksaw.

Crucial Rule: Flood with copious coolant during cutting. Frictional overheating (>150°C) induces artificial tempering, phase transformations, or recrystallization, destroying the authentic microstructure.
2

Progressive Grinding (90° Rotation Technique)

Mount or hold specimen flat against successive waterproof silicon carbide (SiC) emery paper sheets: 1/0 (220 grit) → 2/0 (320 grit) → 3/0 (400 grit) → 4/0 (600/800 grit).

  • Rub the specimen in one direction only with steady, uniform pressure.
  • Between every grit change, wash thoroughly under running tap water and turn the specimen exactly 90°.
  • Grind until all scratches from the prior coarse grade are 100% replaced by parallel fine scratches from the current grade.
3

Disc Cloth Polishing (Mirror Finish)

Transfer specimen to a rotating disc polishing machine covered with synthetic velvet/nylon cloth.

  • Apply fine abrasive suspension: initially 1.0 µm alumina ($Al_2O_3$) or diamond paste, finishing with 0.3 µm or 0.05 µm colloidal silica/alumina.
  • Hold specimen firmly against the spinning wheel while rotating it slowly counter to wheel rotation.
  • Continue until the surface is completely scratch-free, flat, and exhibits a mirror-like reflectance under oblique illumination.
  • Rinse thoroughly with distilled water, swab with ethanol, and blow dry using warm air.
4

Chemical Etching

A polished mirror surface reflects light uniformly without phase contrast. Etching selectively attacks grain boundaries and phases with differing electrochemical potentials.

  • For Carbon Steels: Swab or immerse the polished face in 2% to 3% Nital (2 ml conc. $HNO_3$ in 98 ml ethanol) for 5 to 15 seconds.
  • Watch for the mirror surface to turn slightly dull/frosted silvery gray.
  • Immediately flush under running water, rinse with pure ethanol to arrest etching, and warm-air dry.
Avoid Over-Etching: Over-etching turns the surface black and causes heavy pit corrosion, obliterating delicate phase boundaries.
5

Microscopic Examination

Place specimen on the stage of the inverted or upright metallurgical microscope.

  • Turn on illuminator; align vertical beam through the condenser aperture.
  • Focus initially at low magnification (100X: 10X objective × 10X eyepiece) using coarse adjustment.
  • Switch to 400X / 1000X using fine micrometer focus knob to inspect grain boundaries, pearlite lamellae, or inclusions.
  • Capture digital micrograph or sketch the visual field.

Working Principle of Metallurgical Microscope

Because metals and alloys are completely opaque to visible light, standard biological microscopes (which rely on transmitted light passing through a thin slice) cannot be used. A metallurgical microscope employs a vertical illuminator (semi-silvered prism or plane glass beam-splitter) positioned behind the objective lens.

Total Magnification = Magnification of Objective ($M_{obj}$) × Magnification of Eyepiece ($M_{eye}$)

The incident light travels down through the objective onto the polished specimen surface and is reflected back through the objective to the oculars. Where chemical etching has created microscopic troughs (grain boundaries) or relief differences (phase hardness/corrosion differences), light is scattered away, rendering boundaries and pearlite dark, while flat ferrite grains reflect light directly into the lens and appear bright.

10 High-Yield Viva Voce Questions
Q1

Why cannot a standard biological microscope be used to view metallic specimens?

Answer: Metals and alloys are optically dense and completely opaque; light cannot pass through them. Biological microscopes rely on transmitted light passing through thin, translucent specimens. A metallurgical microscope uses a vertical illuminator that bounces light down through the objective onto the opaque polished surface and captures the reflected light.

Q2

What is the scientific purpose of rotating the specimen by 90° between successive grinding steps?

Answer: Rotating the specimen 90° creates new scratch lines perpendicular to the previous set. This provides an unmistakable visual indicator: you must grind until every single scratch from the previous (coarser) paper is completely removed, ensuring a uniform scratch depth before moving to the next finer grit.

Q3

Why does an unetched, mirror-polished specimen show almost no microstructure under the microscope?

Answer: A perfectly polished specimen behaves like a planar mirror. Incident light is reflected uniformly back across the entire field into the objective, producing a featureless, blindingly bright field (except for cracks, voids, or non-metallic inclusions like manganese sulfides).

Q4

How does chemical etching reveal grain boundaries and distinct phases?

Answer: Grain boundaries have higher atomic disorientation, strain energy, and impurity segregation, making them electrochemically more reactive (anodic) than grain interiors. The etchant preferentially attacks grain boundaries, etching narrow microscopic V-grooves. These grooves scatter reflected light away from the objective lens, making grain boundaries appear as sharp dark lines. Different phases also dissolve at different rates, producing steps and optical contrast.

Q5

What is Nital, and what is its standard concentration for carbon steels?

Answer: Nital is a chemical etchant composed of 2% to 5% concentrated nitric acid ($HNO_3$) in ethanol ($C_2H_5OH$) or methanol. For mild and medium carbon steels, 2% Nital applied for 5 to 15 seconds is standard.

Q6

Describe the visual microstructure of Mild Steel (0.15% C) and identify the white and dark constituents.

Answer: Mild steel consists of approximately 80–85% proeutectoid Ferrite ($\alpha$-iron) which appears as bright/white polygonal grains, and 15–20% Pearlite (eutectoid lamellar mixture of ferrite + cementite), which appears as dark colonies distributed along ferrite boundaries.

Q7

Why does Pearlite appear dark under low magnification even though it is composed of ferrite and cementite?

Answer: Pearlite consists of alternating micro-lamellae of soft ferrite and hard cementite ($Fe_3C$). The etchant attacks ferrite faster than cementite, creating closely spaced microscopic ridges. At low to medium magnification (100X–200X), the interlamellar spacing is smaller than the optical resolution limit, so the ridges scatter light in all directions away from the lens, making the colony appear uniformly dark or brownish-black.

Q8

What happens if you apply excessive pressure or run the disc dry during cloth polishing?

Answer: It causes smearing or burnishing of the surface metal, creating a deformed amorphous Beilby layer that hides true grain boundaries. It also generates severe frictional heat, causes comet-tailing around hard inclusions, and tears the polishing cloth.

Q9

What is an Annealing Twin, and in what crystal structures is it typically observed?

Answer: An annealing twin is a region within a grain bounded by parallel straight boundaries that represents a mirror-image crystal orientation formed during recrystallization. It is widely observed in FCC (Face-Centered Cubic) metals with low stacking-fault energy, notably Copper, 70:30 Brass ($\alpha$-brass), and Austenitic Stainless Steels.

Q10

What is the ASTM grain size formula, and how does grain size affect mechanical strength?

Answer: According to ASTM E112: $N = 2^{G-1}$, where $N$ is the number of grains per square inch at 100X magnification, and $G$ is the ASTM grain size number. A higher $G$ means smaller/finer grains. By the Hall-Petch relationship ($\sigma_y = \sigma_0 + k_y d^{-1/2}$), finer grains provide more grain boundary area, which effectively blocks dislocation movement, increasing both yield strength and fracture toughness simultaneously.

Experiment 02 ASTM E8/E8M Universal Testing Machine

Uniaxial Tension Test of Mild Steel & Aluminium using UTM

Subjecting standardized dogbone specimens to uniaxial quasi-static tensile loading on a 50 kN Universal Testing Machine to determine Young's Modulus ($E$), 0.2% Proof Stress, Ultimate Tensile Strength (UTS), percentage elongation, and energy absorption parameters.

Standard Geometry Flat Specimen: $G=50$ mm, $W=12.5$ mm, $T=3$ mm, $L=200$ mm
Machine Capacity 50 kN Electromechanical / Hydraulic Dual Column UTM
Strain Measurement Clip-on Extensometer ($50$ mm gauge length, ±5 mm travel)
Crosshead Speed 1 to 2 mm/min (Quasi-static strain rate per ASTM E8)

Step-by-Step Practical Execution Protocol

1

Pre-Test Specimen Metrology

Using a digital vernier caliper or micrometer (least count 0.01 mm):

  • Measure initial width $W_0$ and thickness $t_0$ at three locations across the reduced gauge section. Compute mean cross-sectional area: $A_0 = W_0 \times t_0$.
  • Use a gauge marker/punch to accurately mark the standard initial gauge length: $L_0 = 50.0\text{ mm}$.
2

Machine Setup & Alignment

Ensure UTM is powered on and calibrated in computer data acquisition mode.

  • Select wedge grips matching the specimen thickness ($3\text{ mm}$).
  • Insert upper grip first, clamp firmly, then lower crosshead to insert specimen into lower grip.
  • Vertical Alignment: Ensure the specimen is perfectly coaxial with the load train to prevent parasitic bending moments.
3

Extensometer Attachment & Zeroing

Attach the clip-on extensometer knife-edges onto the punched gauge marks on the specimen.

  • Apply a slight pre-load (approx. 50–100 N) to take up slack in grips and load cell.
  • Zero the load sensor ($P=0$) and displacement/strain sensor ($\Delta L = 0$) in the DAQ software.
4

Loading & Extensometer Removal

Start test at constant crosshead speed (e.g. 1.5 mm/min).

  • Observe linear elastic loading (Hooke's law regime).
  • Crucial Action: Once yield point / 0.2% offset strain is passed (or around 1.0–1.5% strain), pause or smoothly detach the extensometer to protect its sensitive spring arms from violent snap-back upon fracture!
  • Resume crosshead movement until ultimate tensile load $P_{max}$ is reached, followed by localized necking and final rupture at $P_f$.
5

Post-Fracture Metrology

Safely retrieve the two fractured halves from the grips.

  • Fit the broken ends tightly together.
  • Measure final gauge length $L_f$ between punch marks.
  • Measure minimum necked dimensions $W_f$ and $t_f$ to compute final neck area $A_f = W_f \times t_f$.
  • Inspect fracture face: check for cup-and-cone morphology (shear lips at 45°).

10 High-Yield Viva Voce Questions
Q1

What is the difference between Engineering Stress-Strain and True Stress-Strain?

Answer: Engineering stress ($\sigma = P/A_0$) and strain ($\epsilon = \Delta L / L_0$) are calculated using the original, undeformed cross-sectional area and gauge length. True stress ($\sigma_t = P/A_i$) and true strain ($\epsilon_t = \ln(L_i/L_0)$) use the instantaneous area and length at that exact moment during deformation. In tension, true stress is always higher than engineering stress due to lateral contraction (Poisson effect and necking).

Q2

What causes the upper and lower yield point phenomenon in low-carbon mild steel?

Answer: It is caused by Cottrell Atmospheres: small interstitial carbon and nitrogen solute atoms diffuse to the dilatational stress fields around dislocations, effectively "pinning" them. At the upper yield point, high stress is required to tear dislocations away from their solute atmosphere or generate new ones. Once unpinned, dislocations glide easily at a significantly lower stress level (the lower yield point), spreading as Lüders bands across the gauge length.

Q3

What is 0.2% Proof Stress and why is it used for Aluminium and other non-ferrous alloys?

Answer: Materials like aluminium alloys, copper, and austenitic stainless steels do not exhibit a distinct yield point; their transition from elastic to plastic behavior is completely smooth and continuous. The 0.2% Proof Stress is the standard engineering convention (ASTM E8): an offset line is drawn starting at $\epsilon = 0.002$ (0.2% plastic strain) parallel to the initial linear elastic slope. The intersection with the stress-strain curve defines the yield strength.

Q4

Why does the engineering stress-strain curve show a drop in stress after UTS, whereas true stress continues to rise?

Answer: Beyond the Ultimate Tensile Strength (UTS), localized necking begins. Because engineering stress divides load by the original, constant area $A_0$, and the load $P$ required to deform the shrinking neck decreases, the curve appears to drop. However, the true local cross-sectional area $A_i$ shrinks even faster than the load drops; hence, the actual true stress ($P/A_i$) continues to escalate monotonically due to strain hardening until final fracture.

Q5

Differentiate between Modulus of Resilience and Modulus of Toughness.

Answer:

  • Modulus of Resilience ($U_r$): The maximum elastic strain energy absorbed per unit volume without permanent deformation (area under the elastic portion of the stress-strain curve up to the proportional/yield limit, approximately $\sigma_y^2 / 2E$).
  • Modulus of Toughness ($U_t$): The total mechanical energy absorbed per unit volume up to complete specimen fracture (the entire area under the stress-strain curve from zero to fracture strain).

Q6

Why are tensile test specimens designed with a reduced gauge section and enlarged ends?

Answer: The serrated wedge grips introduce severe localized contact stresses and notch effects. Enlarged shoulder ends ensure that stresses inside the grips remain low and purely elastic, while stress is maximized uniformly within the reduced central gauge section, guaranteeing that deformation, necking, and rupture occur away from grip interference.

Q7

Why must the clip-on extensometer be detached before specimen fracture?

Answer: Sudden fracture releases massive stored elastic strain energy almost instantaneously. The violent shockwave and physical snap-back of the broken halves will permanently bend the extensometer's delicate flexure arms, chip the sharp knife-edges, or destroy the internal LVDT/strain-gauge bridge.

Q8

Explain the mechanical mechanism of a "cup-and-cone" fracture surface.

Answer: In ductile metals under tension, necking induces a triaxial tensile stress state at the center of the neck. Microvoids nucleate at inclusions, grow, and coalesce to form an internal central fissure perpendicular (90°) to the tensile axis (fibrous central cup). As the fissure reaches the perimeter, final separation occurs by shear sliding along planes of maximum shear stress oriented at 45°, producing characteristic conical shear lips.

Q9

How does increasing the testing crosshead speed (strain rate) influence the tensile test results?

Answer: Increasing the strain rate restricts the thermal activation time available for dislocations to overcome obstacles. Consequently, Yield Strength and Ultimate Tensile Strength increase, while ductility (% elongation and % reduction in area) generally decreases. This is why ASTM E8 strictly specifies standard quasi-static strain rate regimes.

Q10

What is Poisson's ratio ($\nu$), and what are typical values for structural metals?

Answer: Poisson's ratio is the negative ratio of lateral (transverse) strain to longitudinal (axial) strain in the elastic regime: $\nu = -\epsilon_{transverse} / \epsilon_{longitudinal}$. For steel, it is $\approx 0.28–0.30$; for aluminium alloys, $\approx 0.33$. Under fully plastic incompressible flow, effective $\nu = 0.5$.

Fabricating multi-ply glass-fiber reinforced polymer (GFRP) composite laminates and neat polymer coupons using epoxy/polyester resin with hardener (1:2 stoichiometry) to prepare standard specimens for ASTM D256 (Izod) and ASTM D6100 (Charpy) impact testing.

Reinforcement Plies 10 Layers Woven E-Glass Fiber Fabric ($200 \times 200$ mm)
Matrix System Thermoset Epoxy/Polyester + Hardener (Standard 1:2 or 100:50 ratio)
Release Interface PTFE (Teflon) Film / Polyvinyl Alcohol (PVA) + Acetone wash
Cure Regime Ambient Room Temperature (25°C) for 24 Hours under Dead Load

Step-by-Step Practical Execution Protocol

1

Material Preparation & Cutting

Clean work surface thoroughly with acetone. Cut 10 square plies of woven E-glass fiber cloth ($200 \times 200\text{ mm}$) using sharp textile shears, keeping warp and weft fibers aligned.

2

Mold Setup & Release Agent

Place a thick, perfectly flat plate of glass or smooth ceramic tile as the base.

  • Degrease with acetone.
  • Lay a wrinkle-free Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE/Teflon) or Mylar release sheet over the surface to prevent the laminate from bonding irreversibly to the base.
3

Resin-Hardener Stoichiometric Mixing

Weigh the liquid thermoset resin and curing hardener in the designated ratio (1:2 by volume/weight as specified in lab manual) in a disposable cup.

  • Stir gently with a clean spatula for 3 to 5 minutes.
  • Stirring Caution: Avoid rapid whipping, which introduces microscopic air bubbles into the resin matrix.
4

Sequential Lay-up & Compaction Rolling

Apply a thin, uniform resin film onto the PTFE base sheet using a bristle brush.

  • Lay the 1st glass fiber layer over the wet resin; press lightly.
  • Apply more resin-hardener mix over the ply until fully saturated (wet-out).
  • Use a grooved metal or nylon roller, rolling firmly from center to edges to dislodge and expel all trapped air bubbles.
  • Repeat this cycle for all 10 layers, maintaining consistent fiber orientation.
5

Curing & Specimen Extraction

Place top PTFE sheet, followed by a flat top glass tile. Place dead weights (approx. 5–10 kg) on top to maintain uniform compaction pressure.

  • Allow to cure undisturbed at room temperature for 24 hours.
  • Carefully peel off PTFE sheets.
  • Trim irregular flash edges with a diamond saw or waterjet cutter to produce standardized ASTM D256 / ASTM D6100 rectangular specimens.

Composite Mechanics & Role of Components

A composite material is a macroscopic combination of two or more distinct insoluble materials having an interface separating them. It comprises:

  • Matrix Phase (Polymer): Holds fibers together, protects fibers against environmental corrosion and abrasion, transfers shear stresses between fibers, and dictates maximum service temperature.
  • Reinforcement Phase (Glass Fibers): High-strength, high-modulus dispersed phase that bears the principal tensile and bending loads.

10 High-Yield Viva Voce Questions
Q1

What is a Polymer Matrix Composite (PMC) and what are its primary constituents?

Answer: A PMC is an engineered material comprising high-strength, high-stiffness reinforcement fibers (such as E-glass, carbon, or aramid) embedded within a continuous polymer matrix (such as epoxy, polyester, or vinyl ester resin). The fibers carry the primary mechanical loads, while the matrix binds them, transfers shear stress, and protects against environmental degradation.

Q2

What is the chemical role of the hardener in an epoxy system?

Answer: The hardener (typically an aliphatic or aromatic polyamine) acts as a cross-linking curing agent. Its active hydrogen atoms react with the epoxide rings of the resin monomers via an addition reaction, transforming the viscous liquid oligomers into a rigid, three-dimensional infusible thermoset cross-linked polymer network.

Q3

Why must you strictly adhere to the stoichiometric resin-to-hardener ratio?

Answer: Unlike polyester resins where hardener/catalyst can vary, epoxy hardener is a stoichiometric coreactant. Adding too little hardener leaves unreacted epoxy rings, resulting in an under-cured, rubbery, sticky laminate with low $T_g$. Adding excess hardener leaves unreacted amine groups that plasticize the network, causing brittleness, amine blush, and reduced chemical resistance.

Q4

Why is a grooved metal or nylon roller used during hand lay-up?

Answer: The grooved roller serves two essential functions: (1) it forces liquid resin into the tight bundles of glass filaments ensuring complete "wet-out", and (2) its serrated profile mechanically compresses the stack to squeeze out entrapped air voids to the periphery. Unremoved voids act as severe stress raisers and trigger interlaminar shear failure (delamination).

Q5

What is the function of the PTFE (Teflon) sheet and mold release agent?

Answer: Epoxy is an extraordinary structural adhesive with intense bonding affinity to glass, metals, and ceramics. The PTFE sheet (or wax/PVA release agent) provides an extremely low-surface-energy barrier ($<20\text{ mN/m}$) that prevents the polymer matrix from wetting or adhering to the tool plates, allowing damage-free demolding of the cured laminate.

Q6

Differentiate between Thermoset and Thermoplastic polymer matrices.

Answer:

  • Thermosets (Epoxy, Vinyl ester, Polyester): Form permanently cross-linked covalent network structures upon curing. They cannot be remelted or dissolved; heating causes thermal decomposition. They exhibit high stiffness, creep resistance, and thermal stability.
  • Thermoplastics (PEEK, Polypropylene, Nylon): Consist of linear or branched chains held by weak secondary Van der Waals bonds. They can be repeatedly melted, reshaped, and recycled, and offer high fracture toughness.

Q7

What is fiber volume fraction ($V_f$) and why is it generally lower in hand lay-up?

Answer: $V_f$ is the percentage of the composite laminate volume occupied by reinforcing fibers. In hand lay-up, because compaction pressure is low (manual rolling only), excess resin remains trapped between plies, resulting in a moderate $V_f \approx 30–45\%$. Advanced processes like vacuum infusion or autoclave prepreg achieve $V_f \approx 60–70\%$.

Q8

What is composite anisotropy and how is it controlled during lay-up?

Answer: Anisotropy means material properties vary depending on the direction of measurement. A composite is strongest along the longitudinal axis of its fibers. By orienting successive plies at specific angles (e.g. $[0^\circ / 90^\circ / \pm 45^\circ]_s$ quasi-isotropic stacking sequence), engineers can tailor stiffness and strength to match complex multi-axial operational loads.

Q9

Why must composite specimens be cured under a flat plate with dead weights?

Answer: Curing involves an exothermic chemical reaction accompanied by volumetric polymerization shrinkage (typically 2–5%). Applying uniform dead load during the 24-hour cure maintains consolidation pressure, prevents warpage, squeezes out residual resin pools, and guarantees uniform laminate thickness across all four corners.

Q10

What Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) is mandatory during PMC manufacturing and cutting?

Answer: (1) Nitrile gloves: prevent skin contact with uncured liquid amines/epoxies which cause severe contact dermatitis and sensitization; (2) Safety goggles: protect against splashes and high-speed cutting debris; (3) N95/P100 particulate respirator: prevents inhalation of hazardous airborne glass micro-dust and silica fragments generated during trimming and machining.

Experiment 04 ASTM D256 / D6100 Pendulum Impact Tester

Impact Testing of Polymer & Composite Specimens (Charpy vs. Izod)

Subjecting notched polymer and PMC composite specimens to dynamic pendulum impact loading to evaluate absorbed fracture energy and determine impact toughness in J/mm² (or kJ/m²) under high strain rate conditions.

Izod Standard (ASTM D256) 63.5 × 12.7 × Thickness mm, 45° V-notch, 2.54 mm depth
Charpy Standard (ASTM D6100) 127 × 12.7 × Thickness mm, centered 45° V-notch
Support Geometry Izod: Vertical Cantilever | Charpy: Horizontal 3-Point Beam
Pendulum Hammer Calibrated Striking Edge with Dial Gauge / Digital Optical Encoder

Step-by-Step Practical Execution Protocol

1

Machine Zero Calibration (Windage & Friction Check)

Before testing specimens, calibrate the pendulum impact machine:

  • Latch pendulum at its standard release angle (e.g. $\beta_0$).
  • Move the analog friction pointer to the maximum scale value.
  • Release the pendulum without any specimen installed (free swing).
  • Verify the pointer reads exactly 0.0 Joules. If non-zero, record windage/bearing friction loss ($E_0$) to subtract from subsequent readings.
  • Engage mechanical brake to safely halt swinging pendulum.
2

Specimen Metrology & Net Ligament Area

Using a vernier caliper or depth micrometer, measure:

  • Total specimen width $W$ and overall depth $D$.
  • Residual ligament depth remaining under notch root: $h_{net} = D - d_{notch}$.
  • Calculate net cross-sectional fracture area: $A_{net} = W \times h_{net}\text{ (mm}^2\text{)}$.
3

Specimen Clamping & Orientation

Orientation Critical Rule:
  • For CHARPY: Place horizontally on anvil supports. The V-notch MUST face AWAY from the incoming pendulum striker (so notch root is in tension when struck on the opposite face).
  • For IZOD: Clamp vertically in vice. The V-notch MUST face TOWARDS the incoming pendulum striker (striker hits the upper cantilever face above the notch).
4

Execution & Safety Release

Raise and latch pendulum into locked position. Set drag pointer to maximum.

  • Safety Clearance: Verify all personnel stand well outside the swing trajectory plane behind safety cages.
  • Depress safety interlock lever and release pendulum trigger.
  • Pendulum sweeps downward, impacts specimen, fractures it, and swings up to height $h_2$.
  • Immediately apply foot/hand brake to prevent rebound impacts.
5

Data Acquisition & Toughness Calculation

Record indicated absorbed energy $K$ (in Joules). Calculate impact strength:

$$\text{Impact Strength } IS = \frac{K - E_0}{A_{net}} \quad (\text{J/mm}^2 \text{ or } \text{kJ/m}^2)$$

Physical Principles of Pendulum Impact

A pendulum of mass $m$ released from initial height $h_1$ carries potential energy $E_1 = mgh_1$. As it swings through bottom dead center, potential energy converts into maximum kinetic energy. It strikes and fractures the specimen, expending fracture energy $K$, and rises to a lower post-fracture height $h_2$ with residual energy $E_2 = mgh_2$.

$$\text{Absorbed Energy: } K = E_1 - E_2 = mg(h_1 - h_2) = mgL(\cos\beta - \cos\alpha)$$ $$\text{Impact Toughness: } IS = \frac{K}{A_{net}} \quad (\text{kJ/m}^2 \text{ or } \text{J/mm}^2)$$

Function of the Notch: The sharp V-notch creates severe triaxial tension at its root, eliminating shear stress and suppressing plastic yielding, forcing high-rate crack initiation.

10 High-Yield Viva Voce Questions
Q1

What property does an impact test evaluate that a static tensile test cannot reveal?

Answer: The impact test measures dynamic notch toughness: a material's capacity to absorb mechanical energy and resist sudden catastrophic brittle fracture under high strain rates ($\dot{\epsilon} \sim 10^2$ to $10^4\text{ s}^{-1}$) in the presence of severe multi-axial stress concentrations (notches/flaws). Materials that appear ductile under slow quasi-static tension can fail catastrophically in a brittle manner under shock impact.

Q2

State the two primary mechanical differences between Charpy and Izod tests.

Answer:

  • Specimen Support: In Charpy, the specimen is supported horizontally at both ends as a simple beam (three-point bending). In Izod, it is clamped vertically in a vice as a cantilever beam.
  • Notch Direction: In Charpy, the V-notch faces AWAY from the striking hammer (struck on unnotched back face). In Izod, the V-notch faces TOWARDS the striking hammer (struck on same face, 22 mm above vice).

Q3

Why must a V-notch be machined into the impact specimen?

Answer: The sharp notch serves three crucial purposes: (1) it acts as a severe geometrical stress raiser ($K_t \approx 3$ to $5$); (2) it produces a localized triaxial state of tensile stress at its root, suppressing shear stresses and yielding; and (3) it pinpoints the exact fracture plane, preventing random bending deformation.

Q4

Why does a 10-ply Glass Fiber PMC absorb far higher impact energy than a neat polymer?

Answer: Neat polymers fail by single-plane brittle cleavage or crazing with minimal energy dissipation. In contrast, fiber-reinforced composites dissipate immense energy through multiple synergistic micro-mechanical fracture mechanisms: fiber debonding, extensive fiber pull-out against friction, matrix micro-cracking, delamination between plies, and fiber tensile fracture.

Q5

How is windage and bearing friction measured and compensated for in this test?

Answer: By executing a blank (free) swing with no specimen installed. The pendulum is latched and released; the friction pointer records the energy loss ($E_0$) due to air resistance and bearing friction. If non-zero, this correction factor is subtracted from the gross dial reading: $K_{true} = K_{indicated} - E_0$.

Q6

What is the Ductile-to-Brittle Transition Temperature (DBTT) and what crystal structure exhibits it?

Answer: DBTT is the temperature range where a material undergoes a drastic drop in impact energy absorption from ductile tearing to brittle cleavage. It is characteristic of BCC (Body-Centered Cubic) metals (like ferritic mild steel) and certain polymers due to strong temperature sensitivity of Peierls-Nabarro lattice friction stress. FCC metals (like Al, Cu, Austenitic steel) do not exhibit DBTT and remain tough even at cryogenic temperatures.

Q7

What happens if the specimen does not break completely during the impact swing?

Answer: This is termed a "hinged break" or "non-break". According to ASTM standards, the recorded energy must be reported with an explicit qualifier ("NB - Non-Break" or "% Hinge"), because dragging an unbroken ductile specimen through the anvils expends parasitic friction energy, making the apparent absorbed energy invalid for standard impact toughness comparisons.

Q8

Why must you immediately apply the pendulum brake after the forward fracture swing?

Answer: On its backward swing, the heavy pendulum hammer can strike the loose fractured halves or catch on the anvil, launching high-velocity metal/composite fragments outward into the laboratory or damaging the precision striking edge.

Q9

What units are used to express impact properties in lab reports?

Answer:

  • Absorbed Energy: Joules ($J$) or foot-pounds ($ft\cdot lbf$).
  • Impact Strength / Notch Toughness: Energy normalized by net cross-sectional area: $\text{kJ/m}^2$ or $\text{J/mm}^2$ ($1\text{ J/mm}^2 = 1000\text{ kJ/m}^2$), or energy normalized per unit specimen width: $\text{J/m}$ (frequently used in ASTM D256 for plastics).

Q10

How does notch radius affect the measured impact energy?

Answer: Impact energy is extremely sensitive to notch root radius ($r$). By Inglis theory of stress concentration ($\sigma_{max} = 2\sigma_0\sqrt{a/r}$), a sharper notch (smaller radius) sharply increases stress concentration, promoting brittle failure and reducing measured impact energy. An oversized or worn notch cutter root radius falsely inflates toughness.

Q1

How do you calculate the least count of a travelling microscope?

Answer: The Least Count ($LC$) is the smallest displacement measurable by the vernier scale. It is calculated as: $$LC = \frac{\text{Value of one smallest division on main scale (SMD)}}{\text{Total number of divisions on sliding vernier scale (VSD)}}$$ For our instrument: $SMD = 0.5\text{ mm}$ and $VSD = 50$, giving $LC = 0.5 / 50 = 0.01\text{ mm}$ ($10\text{ µm}$).

Q2

Why must crack length be measured at three distinct locations across the specimen thickness?

Answer: Due to stress state variation across the thickness: the mid-plane experiences severe lateral constraint (high triaxial stress / plane strain condition), whereas the outer free surfaces experience unconstrained plane stress. Consequently, the fatigue crack grows faster in the interior than at the edges, producing a curved, bowed crack front (termed crack tunneling). ASTM E399 requires measuring at $B/4$, $B/2$, and $3B/4$ to calculate a representative effective average crack length.

Q3

How do you visually distinguish the fatigue pre-crack zone from the fast-fracture ($K_{IC}$) zone?

Answer:

  • Fatigue Zone: Very flat, burnished, velvety-smooth, highly reflective. The repeated cyclic opening and closing creates microscopic rub marks and striations.
  • Fast Fracture ($K_{IC}$) Zone: Much rougher, dull, fibrous, faceted crystalline appearance, showing chaotic micro-cleavage or tearing topography.
The sharp boundary line between them marks the transition from stable sub-critical fatigue growth to unstable fast rupture.

Q4

Why cannot we test fracture toughness directly using a machined V-notch without fatigue pre-cracking?

Answer: The sharpest machining cutter or EDM wire leaves a finite root radius ($\ge 0.05$ to $0.1\text{ mm}$), which blunts the crack tip, lowers localized triaxial stress concentration, and permits excessive plastic yielding. This artificially inflates the apparent fracture toughness. A fatigue pre-crack generates a truly atomically sharp crack tip ($r \to 0$), satisfying linear elastic singularity assumptions.

Q5

What is a Compact Tension (CT) specimen and what are its key geometric parameters?

Answer: A standard ASTM E399 fracture test coupon featuring two pin-loading holes ($6.35\text{ mm}$ dia) and a machined V-notch with chevron starter crack. For our specimen: width $W = 31.84\text{ mm}$, height $H = 30.58\text{ mm}$, thickness $B = 12.65\text{ mm}$, notch angle $85^\circ$, and nominal starter notch depth $p = 1.3\text{ mm}$.

Q6

What is Plane Strain Fracture Toughness ($K_{IC}$)?

Answer: $K_{IC}$ is an intrinsic material property representing the minimum critical stress intensity factor required for unstable crack propagation under Mode-I (opening mode) under conditions of maximum triaxial constraint (thick specimens in plane strain where out-of-plane strain $\epsilon_z = 0$). Units: $\text{MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}}$ or $\text{N}\cdot\text{mm}^{-3/2}$.

Q7

Why must you avoid touching the fracture surface with your fingers?

Answer: Human skin secretes natural oils, moisture, fatty acids, and sodium chloride. When deposited on bare aluminum or steel fracture surfaces, they induce rapid chemical staining, corrosion pitting, and obscure the subtle microscopic boundary between the fatigue pre-crack zone and fast fracture surface.

Q8

What is Parallax Error and how is it prevented when taking vernier scale readings?

Answer: Parallax error occurs when an observer views graduated scale divisions from an angle rather than perpendicular (90°) to the scale plane, causing apparent displacement between overlapping vernier and main scale lines. It is eliminated by positioning your eye directly in line with the scale normal or using the built-in magnifying loupe.

Q9

What are the three fundamental crack opening modes in fracture mechanics?

Answer:

  • Mode I (Opening Mode): Tensile stress normal to the crack plane pulls crack faces apart (most common and dangerous in engineering).
  • Mode II (Sliding / In-Plane Shear Mode): Shear stresses act parallel to crack plane and perpendicular to crack front.
  • Mode III (Tearing / Anti-Plane Shear Mode): Shear stresses act parallel to both crack plane and crack front.

Q10

What alloy is Aluminum 7075-T6 and why is it used in aerospace applications?

Answer: 7075 is a precipitation-hardened aluminum alloy whose primary alloying element is Zinc (~5.6–6.1% Zn) along with Magnesium and Copper. The T6 temper (solution heat treated and artificially aged) produces fine $\eta'$ ($MgZn_2$) precipitates, providing an exceptional yield strength ($\approx 500\text{ MPa}$) comparable to structural steels at one-third the density, making it a benchmark aerospace structural alloy.

10 High-Yield Viva Voce Questions
Q1

What is fatigue failure and why is it considered the most dangerous mode of structural failure?

Answer: Fatigue is progressive, localized structural damage that occurs when a component is subjected to cyclic, fluctuating, or repeated stresses whose amplitudes are well below the static yield strength of the material. It is exceptionally dangerous because it occurs suddenly with virtually zero gross macroscopic plastic warning or visible necking, accounting for over 80–90% of all in-service mechanical failures.

Q2

What is the stress ratio ($R$) in a rotating bending fatigue test?

Answer: In rotating bending, the top surface experiences peak compression ($-\sigma_a$) while the bottom surface experiences equal peak tension ($+\sigma_a$). As the shaft rotates 360°, every surface fiber undergoes sinusoidal alternating stress with zero mean stress ($\sigma_m = 0$). Hence: $$R = \frac{\sigma_{min}}{\sigma_{max}} = \frac{-\sigma_a}{+\sigma_a} = -1.0$$

Q3

What is the Endurance Limit (Fatigue Limit) and what materials possess it?

Answer: The endurance limit ($\sigma_e$) is the threshold stress amplitude below which a material can withstand an infinite number of stress cycles ($>10^7$) without failure. It is possessed by ferrous metals (steels, cast irons) and titanium alloys due to interstitial atom pinning of dislocations. Non-ferrous metals like aluminum and copper do not have an endurance limit.

Q4

Why does the central reduced section of the specimen experience pure bending?

Answer: The loading arrangement uses two identical hangers placed symmetrically between rotary bearing pivots. By standard shear-force and bending-moment diagrams for four-point symmetric loading, the transverse shear force ($V = dM/dx$) in the central span between pivots is identically zero, resulting in a strictly constant, uniform bending moment ($M = W \cdot L / 2$) with no parasitic transverse shear stresses.

Q5

Why is surface finish extremely critical in fatigue life testing?

Answer: Under bending, bending stress is maximal at the extreme outer fibers ($y = \pm d/2$) and zero at the neutral axis. Furthermore, fatigue micro-cracks almost always initiate at surface stress concentrations (machining grooves, scratches, corrosion pits, burrs). A poor surface finish can slash fatigue life by 50% to 80% compared to a mirror-polished specimen.

Q6

What are Beachmarks and Striations on a fatigue fracture surface?

Answer:

  • Beachmarks (Clam-shell marks): Macroscopic concentric rings visible to the unaided eye that radiate outward from the initiation origin, representing operational pauses, load spectrum changes, or intermittent running periods.
  • Striations: Microscopic parallel ripples visible only under Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), where each individual ripple represents the advance of the crack front by one single load cycle.

Q7

What is a "Run-out" in fatigue testing?

Answer: A test specimen that survives a predefined target threshold number of cycles (typically $10^7$ cycles for steels) without fracturing. On the S-N plot, a run-out data point is denoted by a small horizontal arrow pointing to the right, indicating that the true life exceeds that value.

Q8

How does rotating speed (rpm) affect the measured fatigue limit?

Answer: In inert ambient conditions, test frequencies between 10 Hz and 100 Hz (e.g. 3500 rpm = 58.3 Hz) have a negligible effect on fatigue life for structural steels. However, at extreme frequencies (>200 Hz), internal hysteretic damping causes significant self-heating, which can prematurely degrade fatigue strength.

Q9

What are the three main engineering methods used to improve fatigue life?

Answer:

  • Surface Compressive Residual Stresses: Shot peening, laser shock peening, or surface rolling to prevent crack opening.
  • Surface Hardening & Chemical Treatments: Carburizing, nitriding, induction hardening to resist slip-band formation.
  • Geometrical Optimization: Providing generous transition fillet radii, eliminating keyway undercuts, and fine mirror polishing.

Q10

What is Basquin's equation for the high-cycle fatigue regime?

Answer: Basquin's power-law relationship models the finite-life linear portion of the log-log S-N curve: $$\sigma_a = \sigma_f' (2 N_f)^b$$ where $\sigma_a$ is the alternating stress amplitude, $\sigma_f'$ is the fatigue strength coefficient, $2N_f$ is reversals to failure, and $b$ is the fatigue strength exponent (typically $-0.05$ to $-0.12$ for structural metals).

Q1

Define Hardness and differentiate between Macrohardness and Microhardness.

Answer: Hardness is the mechanical resistance of a material to localized permanent plastic deformation, indentation, scratching, or abrasion.

  • Macrohardness ($P \ge 1\text{ kgf}$): Measures bulk average hardness over large grains/phases using standard loads (Brinell, Rockwell, Macro-Vickers).
  • Microhardness ($P < 1\text{ kgf}$, typically $10–1000\text{ gf}$): Uses tiny loads producing microscopic indents suitable for thin foils, surface electroplatings, nitrided case depths, and individual microstructural phases (Micro-Vickers, Knoop).

Q2

Why is the angle between opposite faces of the Vickers diamond pyramid chosen as 136°?

Answer: In the Brinell test, the most accurate, linear, and reproducible results occur when the indentation diameter $d$ is approximately $0.375$ of the ball diameter $D$ ($d/D = 0.375$). The tangents drawn to a sphere at the edges of this ideal indent subtend an included angle of exactly 136°. By using a diamond pyramid with this angle, Vickers readings match Brinell numbers over the standard Brinell range.

Q3

What is the supreme advantage of the Vickers test over the Brinell test?

Answer: Geometrical similarity: A pyramid indenter produces indentations that are geometrically similar regardless of depth or test load. Therefore, the Vickers hardness value ($HV$) is largely independent of applied load. Furthermore, because it uses an ultra-hard diamond, Vickers can measure everything from soft lead to hard ceramic coatings on a single continuous, non-arbitrary scale, whereas a steel Brinell ball deforms at $>450\text{ BHN}$.

Q4

How does the Rockwell hardness test fundamentally differ from Vickers and Brinell?

Answer: Vickers and Brinell measure the surface area / width of indentation optically via a microscope after load removal. Rockwell measures the net permanent depth of penetration directly using a displacement dial gauge under differential loading (minor preload of 10 kgf followed by major load of 60/100/150 kgf). Rockwell requires zero optical measurements, making it fast and operator-independent for production inspection.

Q5

Explain Rockwell scales HRC and HRB.

Answer:

  • HRC (Rockwell C): Uses a $120^\circ$ spheroconical diamond cone (Brale indenter) with a $150\text{ kgf}$ major load. Used for hardened steels, tool steels, and quenched alloys ($20–70\text{ HRC}$).
  • HRB (Rockwell B): Uses a $1/16\text{ inch}$ ($1.588\text{ mm}$) diameter hardened steel/carbide ball with a $100\text{ kgf}$ major load. Used for softer metals: annealed mild steel, brass, copper, and aluminium alloys ($0–100\text{ HRB}$).

Q6

What is Knoop hardness and when is it preferred over Vickers?

Answer: Knoop testing uses an elongated rhombohedral diamond pyramid with angles of $172^\circ 30'$ and $130^\circ$, producing a rhombic indentation where the long diagonal is ~7 times the short diagonal and the depth is only ~1/30 of the length. It is preferred when testing extremely thin surface coatings, brittle ceramics/glasses (where square Vickers indenters cause edge chipping/cracking), and individual microstructural grains.

Q7

Why must consecutive hardness indentations be spaced at least 3 to 5 diagonal lengths apart?

Answer: The plastic indentation process displaces metal, inducing a surrounding zone of severe plastic strain and work hardening (strain hardening). If a second indentation is placed too close to a previous one, it penetrates already strain-hardened material, yielding a falsely elevated hardness number.

Q8

What causes "piling-up" or "sinking-in" around Vickers indentations?

Answer:

  • Piling-up: Heavily cold-worked metals have little remaining work-hardening capacity; displaced metal flows upward around the indenter faces, causing barrel-shaped convex indent sides.
  • Sinking-in: Fully annealed metals have high work-hardening capacity; subsurface material strain-hardens and pulls surrounding surface downward, causing pincushion-shaped concave indent sides.

Q9

Why is the Vickers hardness of Mild Steel higher than Aluminium alloy?

Answer: Mild steel (~130–180 HV) has stronger interatomic bonding, a higher shear modulus, and a harder pearlitic second phase ($Fe_3C$) that effectively hinders dislocation motion. Pure/annealed aluminium alloys (~40–80 HV) have lower shear modulus and FCC slip systems that activate easily at much lower shear stresses under the indenter.

Q10

What is the empirical rule-of-thumb relationship between Vickers Hardness and Ultimate Tensile Strength for steel?

Answer: For carbon and low-alloy steels, empirical correlation (Tabor's relationship based on constraint factor $\approx 3$): $$\text{UTS (in MPa)} \approx 3.2 \times HV \quad (\text{or } \text{UTS (in MPa)} \approx 3.45 \times BHN)$$ For example, steel with $150\text{ HV}$ has an estimated UTS of $3.2 \times 150 \approx 480\text{ MPa}$.

10 High-Yield Viva Voce Questions
Q1

Define Heat Treatment and state its three sequential operational stages.

Answer: Heat treatment is an operation or combination of operations involving the heating, holding (soaking), and cooling of a metal or alloy in the solid state to alter its internal microstructure and tailor its mechanical, physical, or chemical properties without altering overall component geometry. Its three stages are: (1) Heating to target austenitizing temperature; (2) Soaking/Holding for phase homogenization; (3) Controlled Cooling at predetermined rates.

Q2

What is the difference between Annealing and Normalizing cooling rates and microstructures?

Answer:

  • Full Annealing: Uses very slow cooling inside the turned-off furnace (~10–30°C/hr). Provides ample time for carbon diffusion, producing coarse equiaxed ferrite and coarse pearlite lamellae. Yields lowest hardness, minimum internal stress, and maximum ductility.
  • Normalizing: Uses faster cooling in ambient still air. Higher undercooling suppresses diffusion distance, producing fine equiaxed ferrite and fine, tightly spaced pearlite lamellae. Yields a more uniform grain structure with higher yield strength and hardness than annealed steel.

Q3

What is Martensite, how is it formed, and why is it so hard and brittle?

Answer: Martensite is a supersaturated interstitial solid solution of carbon in body-centered tetragonal (BCT) iron. It forms through a diffusionless, athermal shear transformation when austenite is quenched faster than the Critical Cooling Rate (CCR). Carbon atoms become trapped in octahedral interstitial sites, distorting the cubic lattice into tetragonal symmetry. This severe lattice strain, combined with an enormous density of transformation dislocations and internal twin interfaces, makes dislocation motion nearly impossible, resulting in extreme hardness and brittleness.

Q4

Why must as-quenched steel always undergo Tempering?

Answer: As-quenched martensite is severely brittle and riddled with high internal residual quenching stresses that can cause spontaneous quench-cracking or catastrophic failure under service shocks. Tempering (reheating below $Ac_1$, typically 200°C–650°C) allows trapped carbon to precipitate as submicroscopic iron carbides ($\epsilon$-carbide and $Fe_3C$), relieving internal residual stresses, reducing tetragonality, and dramatically restoring fracture toughness and ductility.

Q5

Why does mild steel (0.15–0.20% C) show only a modest increase in hardness after water quenching compared to high carbon steel?

Answer: Martensite hardness is governed directly by interstitial carbon content. At only 0.18% C, the tetragonality ($c/a$ ratio) of the BCT lattice is very small ($c/a \approx 1.01$), forming low-hardness "lath martensite". Furthermore, because low carbon steel has poor hardenability, the Critical Cooling Rate is extremely high; even water quenching often fails to suppress non-martensitic transformation, leaving a substantial proportion of soft proeutectoid ferrite in the microstructure.

Q6

Define Upper Critical ($Ac_3$) and Lower Critical ($Ac_1$) temperatures for hypoeutectoid steel.

Answer:

  • Lower Critical Temperature ($Ac_1 \approx 723^\circ–727^\circ\text{C}$): The eutectoid isotherm at which pearlite begins transforming into austenite upon heating.
  • Upper Critical Temperature ($Ac_3$): The temperature above which the remaining proeutectoid ferrite completely dissolves, converting the steel into 100% single-phase homogeneous Austenite ($\gamma$).
The prefix 'c' stands for chauffage (heating).

Q7

Why is a Muffle Furnace used rather than an open flame furnace for lab heat treatment?

Answer: In a muffle furnace, the internal heating chamber is completely isolated ("muffled") from direct contact with electrical resistance elements or combustion gases. This ensures: (1) highly uniform radiant heating across the chamber without hot-spots; (2) precise digital PID temperature control via thermocouples; and (3) minimization of severe surface oxidation, scaling, and surface carbon burnout (decarburization).

Q8

What is the Critical Cooling Rate (CCR)?

Answer: The Critical Cooling Rate is the minimum rate of continuous cooling required to just bypass the "nose" of the Time-Temperature-Transformation (TTT) or Continuous Cooling Transformation (CCT) diagram, completely suppressing diffusion-controlled transformations into ferrite, pearlite, or bainite, and ensuring 100% transformation into martensite.

Q9

Why must specimen transfer from furnace to quench tank take less than 2 seconds?

Answer: Due to low thermal mass in small lab coupons, any hesitation during transfer in room air allows the specimen temperature to drop into the $700^\circ–550^\circ\text{C}$ range (the TTT pearlite nose). If transformation initiates before entering the water, coarse ferrite and pearlite will precipitate, preventing full martensitic hardening.

Q10

What is Temper Brittleness and in what temperature range does it occur?

Answer: Temper brittleness is a sharp loss in impact toughness that occurs when certain alloy steels are tempered within or slowly cooled through the temperature range of $375^\circ\text{C}$ to $575^\circ\text{C}$. It is caused by the segregation of tramp impurity elements (phosphorus, antimony, arsenic, tin) along prior austenite grain boundaries. It is prevented by adding Molybdenum (~0.2–0.5% Mo) or rapidly water quenching from the tempering temperature.
